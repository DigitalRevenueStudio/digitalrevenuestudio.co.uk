How it came about

The Digital Income Experiment was set up to test ways of earning online and to publish the real numbers, including the zeros. We built its brand, website and first articles. One idea ran through all of it: before chasing new income, check you are not already losing money you do not need to lose.

The council tax story started with a conversation with a neighbour. It got the experiment’s founder wondering whether their own band was right, and it took about ten minutes on GOV.UK to look up the houses on their own street. Several homes of the same size, type and age were in Band C. Theirs was in Band D.

That one check started a process that ended with £1,200 paid back and a lower bill every year since. We helped write it up for the Digital Income Experiment, then turned it into a plain-English guide that was shared in r/UKPersonalFinance. It travelled far further than anything we had published before, with no promotion and no existing audience. That reach shaped a lot of what we did next, including the post that led to the Whiteout Survival wiki.

How council tax banding works

Every home in England is placed in a council tax band from A to H. The bands are based on what the property would have been worth on 1 April 1991, not what it is worth today. Many homes were banded quickly in the early 1990s without an individual survey, and some were put in the wrong band.

Nobody checks this for you. Your council will not write to say you have been overpaying. If your band is wrong, you have to notice it and ask the Valuation Office Agency (VOA) to review it.

Wales uses different bands based on 2003 values, and Scotland has its own system. The process below describes England, where this challenge took place.

The two checks that came first

A band review can go up as well as down, and there have been cases where a challenge led to neighbouring homes being moved up instead. So nothing was submitted until two separate checks both pointed the same way.

Check 1: the neighbours check

The postcode went into the GOV.UK council tax band search, which lists every home nearby with its band. From that list came the homes that genuinely matched: the same type, a similar size and age, on the same street or very close by. Around ten or eleven were checked, because one or two comparisons can be a coincidence but a consistent pattern is evidence. Each address and its band was written down, and that list became the core of the challenge.

Check 2: the 1991 value check

Next came an estimate of what the home would have been worth in 1991. A known sale price for the property from a house price site went into the Nationwide House Price Index calculator, which works a price back to an earlier date. Nationwide is clear that the calculator is a guide rather than a valuation, and it was treated that way, but the result sat comfortably inside the Band C range in the table above rather than Band D.

With both checks pointing to Band C, there was enough confidence to go ahead.

How the challenge was submitted

  1. Gathered the evidence. The list of comparable homes with their bands and a short note on why each one was comparable, plus the 1991 estimate as supporting evidence.
  2. Wrote a formal letter to the VOA. It named the current band, the band the evidence supported, and the comparable properties. No solicitor and no claims company. The template below is based on that letter, adapted so anyone can fill it in.
  3. Submitted it and waited. You can ask for a review through GOV.UK or by post. GOV.UK explains when you can ask for a review and what evidence the VOA wants, so check the current guidance before you send anything.
  4. Kept records. A copy of everything sent was saved with the date, which mattered because the wait was long.

The outcome

The review took more than two years from submission to the final decision. That is the part most write-ups leave out. Nothing happens quickly, and there is a long stretch where you hear very little.

When the decision came, the home moved from Band D to Band C, backdated to when the founder became liable for council tax at the address. The council’s default was to put the overpayment on the account as a credit against future bills. Instead, the founder phoned the council and asked for a full settlement. They agreed on the call, and £1,200 was paid back into the bank account within about two weeks.

What we learned

  • The ten-minute check is worth doing for everyone. Most people never look. If your neighbours' similar homes are in the same band as yours, you have lost ten minutes. If they are in a lower band, it may be worth a closer look.
  • Evidence beats hope. A pattern across ten or so genuinely comparable homes is far stronger than one neighbour's band. Do both checks before you submit.
  • Submit early, then be patient. Because a review can take a long time, the sooner it is in, the sooner the clock starts.
  • You do not have to accept a credit. If the decision goes your way, ask the council for a full settlement rather than a deduction from future bills. Here, one phone call did it.
  • You do not need to pay anyone. The process is free. Be wary of companies that charge a fee or take a cut for something you can do yourself with a letter.
  • Know the risk. A band can go up as well as down. Only go ahead if your evidence is clear and consistent.

Common questions

How do I find my council tax band?

Search your postcode on the GOV.UK council tax band search. It shows your band and the bands of other homes nearby, which is also where the neighbours check starts.

Can challenging my band make it go up?

Yes. The VOA can decide a band is too low as well as too high, and neighbouring homes can be reviewed too. That is why this challenge only went ahead after both checks pointed the same way.

How long does a council tax band review take?

It varies. This one took more than two years from submission to the final decision, so submit as early as you can and keep a record of what you sent.

Will I get a refund if my band is lowered?

If your band is reduced, the overpayment is normally worked out back to when you became liable at that address. The council may add it to your account as a credit, but it is worth asking for a full settlement instead. That request led to £1,200 being paid back within about two weeks.

Do I need a solicitor or a claims company?

No. This challenge was made with a single letter and cost nothing. The template below is based on it.

The free council tax challenge letter template

This letter is based on the one used in this challenge, adapted so you can fill in your own details and comparable properties. It is free, with no sign-up. Copy it, print it, or use it as a starting point for the GOV.UK online form.

How to use this template

  1. Replace every highlighted placeholder with your own details.
  2. Research comparable properties on your street using the gov.uk council tax band search.
  3. Estimate your home’s 1991 value (a house price index calculator can work back from a known sale price) and add it as supporting evidence.
  4. Send the completed letter to the Valuation Office Agency via gov.uk or by post.
  5. Keep a copy for your records and note the date you submitted.
