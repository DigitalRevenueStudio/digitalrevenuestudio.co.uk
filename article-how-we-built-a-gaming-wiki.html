How we built a free gaming wiki from one Reddit comment

We set out to sell a Whiteout Survival guide. A single comment on Reddit changed the plan. This is the full story, what the wiki has become, and the practical lessons if you want to build a gaming wiki of your own.

Case Study and Guide · Whiteout Survival Wiki · Live and growing

The Whiteout Survival wiki was never part of the plan. Digital Revenue Studio set out to help brands build useful websites and content, not to run a gaming reference site. The wiki exists because a Reddit post travelled further than we expected, and because one player told us, in a single sentence, what the community actually needed.

This article tells that story from the start, explains what the wiki is today, and then turns it into practical advice. If you are thinking about building a wiki for a game you play, the second half is for you.

The post that showed us what Reddit could do

Before there was any gaming project, we helped the Digital Income Experiment turn a council tax band challenge into a plain-English guide and letter template. It was shared in r/UKPersonalFinance and went viral, with no promotion behind it and no existing audience. It was simply a useful answer, posted in the right community at the right time. You can read that story in how one council tax challenge got £1,200 back.

The reach was the lesson. Reddit rewards genuine contribution, and a post that solves a real problem can travel a very long way. We started looking at where else a useful guide might do the same.

The original plan: a paid guide

One of those places was Whiteout Survival, a mobile strategy game we play every day. The plan was straightforward: a comprehensive paid guide for free-to-play players, covering heroes, events, research, the economy and everything else the game does not explain clearly. It would cost £9.99 as a download.

The thinking made sense on paper. We had spent hundreds of hours in the game, made most of the mistakes it is possible to make, and worked out what actually moved progression. That knowledge had value, and a paid guide felt like the obvious format. In January 2026 we posted an early version of the hero guide to r/whiteoutsurvival as a free sample, to see whether anyone wanted it.

17,000 views and one comment that mattered

The post did far better than we expected: 17,000 views, 58 upvotes, 63 comments and two awards. The comments covered everything from hero questions and build order debates to event tips and corrections. One of them, from a player called ChandlerOG, stopped us mid-scroll.

ChandlerOG • r/whiteoutsurvival

"There's hundreds of guides like this online. Just not put into a single wiki."

Three upvotes. One sentence. Completely accurate. We replied “On it. Challenge accepted.” and meant it. The gap was not more guides. The gap was one place: organised, consistent and written from a genuine free-to-play perspective.

Why we chose free over paid

The change of direction was not automatic. A paid guide is a product with clear economics. A free wiki is a long-term community resource with a very different kind of value, and much less certainty about money.

What decided it was looking at who was actually engaging. The people in those 63 comments were not shopping for a product. They were sharing knowledge, correcting each other and asking questions that no single guide had answered cleanly. A wiki served them far better than a paywall ever would.

The decision: the wiki and every guide in it would be free, permanently. No sign-up walls, no paywalls and no content locked behind a purchase. Built by players, for players, and shaped by community input.

We built the first version with a handful of guides and a gift code tracker, posted back to Reddit asking what was missing, and kept building whatever the community asked for. Later we went further and retired the pages that had still pointed readers towards the original paid guide, because a free wiki that keeps nudging you to buy something is not really free.

What the community built

Calling it “our” wiki would not be accurate. Its shape is the direct result of player input at every stage. ChandlerOG’s comment started it, and we later reached out to him directly. He shared the wiki with his alliance, WAO in State 1389, and feedback from players at that level made the guides more useful for everyone.

Slick78 and JayTheConqueror came in early, checking facts and sending corrections before guides went fully live. One-Temperature-1025 caught four significant errors in the hero priority guide within hours of a Reddit post and wrote each one out in full. CestBritneyPute flagged a problem with the Bear Hunt hero table that we had missed across several revisions. Every correction made the wiki more accurate for the next player who reads it.

What the wiki is today

Ten months on, the wiki has grown well beyond that first handful of pages. Everything is still free, with no login and no email address needed to read it.

30 guides

Beginner guides, event walkthroughs and strategy guides, from the first seven days to SvS preparation, Foundry Battle and Troop Mastery.

60 hero pages

A hero guide by generation, from Gen 1 to Gen 18, with star targets for every budget and a page for each hero.

Free tools

The Hero Advisor and Bear Generator run in the browser, alongside alliance templates and cheat sheets.

Gift code tracker

Every current code with a clear status label, plus a record of more than 80 expired codes.

Two pages matter more than they look. The community review page shows every guide’s review status and explains how players can send corrections and get credited. The checks and corrections page records what we have confirmed on our own screens, what we corrected and when.

The wiki is also where most of the site’s search traffic comes from. In the four weeks to 8 November 2026, the R4 resources page alone brought 176 visitors from Google, and the experts guide brought 148. None of that came from advertising. It came from answering the questions players were already searching for.

How to build a gaming wiki: what we learned

If you play a game with a community that keeps asking the same questions, you can build something like this. Here is what we would tell you, based on doing it.

1. Start where the players already are

Find the subreddit, Discord or forum where your game’s players ask questions, and spend time answering them before you build anything. The questions that come up again and again are your first pages. A free sample post, like our hero guide, is the cheapest possible test of demand, and the comments will tell you what to build.

2. Decide the structure before you write

A wiki is only useful if people can find things. Group pages into a few clear hubs, such as beginner guides, events, heroes, economy and alliance tools, and give every page the same shape. Ours all open with a quick summary, show a review status and say when they were last checked, so a reader knows straight away what they are looking at.

3. Keep the hosting simple

You do not need wiki software. Our wiki is plain HTML pages hosted free on Cloudflare Pages, on our own domain, deployed from GitHub. It is fast, it costs nothing to host and Google indexed it from the start. The trade-off is that every update is a file change rather than a quick edit in a browser, so it suits someone who is comfortable with a simple build process. Hosted wiki platforms are quicker to start, but you give up control of the design, the adverts and the domain.

4. Write from your own screens

Game information goes out of date quickly, and copied numbers spread mistakes. Check what you can in the game yourself, say clearly when something comes from published information or community reports, and avoid absolutes. Our gift codes carry labels such as Verified active and Reported active for exactly this reason.

5. Build a correction loop on day one

Make it easy to tell you something is wrong: a feedback form, an email address and a promise that a real person reads it. Credit people who help, if they want to be credited, and fix things quickly. Players forgive mistakes. They do not forgive mistakes that stay up for weeks.

6. Add tools only when a question keeps repeating

Our Hero Advisor exists because “should I build this hero?” was the most common question we saw. The Bear Generator exists because alliances kept asking which heroes to send to Bear Hunt. A tool that answers a repeated question is used every day. A tool built because it seemed clever is not.

7. Keep it free, and be honest about money

Trust is the whole asset. Keep every guide free to read, and if you want support, make it optional and say so plainly. Ours is a Ko-fi link and an email list for gift code alerts. Never put a fake urgency banner or a paid upsell in the middle of a free guide.

8. Respect the game and its owners

Say clearly that the wiki is an independent fan resource and not affiliated with the developer. Use game names to identify what you are writing about, avoid passing off official artwork as your own, and follow the community’s rules when you share links.

9. Plan for the upkeep

A gaming wiki is never finished. Events change, new heroes arrive and gift codes expire within days. Decide how often you will check pages, keep a list of what needs re-checking after each update, and accept that a smaller wiki you keep accurate is worth more than a big one that quietly goes stale.

Mistakes we made along the way

  • Stating things too firmly. Early guides used absolutes and exact figures that turned out to vary by state and server age. We now use approximate language and explain why advice might differ.
  • Labelling every code “active”. We could not re-test every gift code, so we split them into verified and reported labels and said so openly.
  • Selling inside a free resource. Some early pages pointed to the paid guide. We retired them, because they undermined the reason people trusted the wiki.
  • Underestimating the upkeep. The game updates constantly. Building the wiki was the easy part; keeping it right is the real work.

Common questions

Do I need coding skills to build a gaming wiki?

Not necessarily. Hosted wiki platforms let you start without code. We chose plain HTML on Cloudflare Pages for speed, cost and control, which needs basic comfort with files and a simple deployment process.

How much does it cost to run?

Hosting on Cloudflare Pages is free for a site like ours. The main costs are a domain name and your time. The time is by far the bigger of the two.

How do you get the first readers?

By answering questions where players already gather and sharing a genuinely useful page when it fits the conversation. Our first readers came from a single Reddit post, not from adverts or search.

Is the Whiteout Survival wiki official?

No. It is an independent fan resource and is not affiliated with or endorsed by Century Games.
